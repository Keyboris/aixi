\begin{abstract}
This report documents the design and implementation of an AIXItl agent -- a
computable approximation of Hutter's universal artificial intelligence agent AIXI --
together with the machinery required to build it: Turing and universal Turing machines,
a bounded Kolmogorov complexity calculator, a Solomonoff universal predictor, and an
expectimax planner operating over a toy environment. The work is organised as a sequence
of self-contained stages, each of which is described in its own section.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:introduction}

AIXI is a universal artificial intelligence agent proposed by Marcus Hutter. At its core,
it extends Solomonoff's theory of universal induction -- which provides an optimal method
for predicting the next output of any unknown computable process -- to the case of an
active agent that takes actions and receives rewards. Classical reinforcement learning
algorithms rely on structural assumptions about the environment, such as the Markov
property, stationarity, or a known state space. AIXI requires none of these. It instead
maintains a Bayesian mixture over all computable environments, weighted by their
Kolmogorov complexity, and selects actions by planning with an expectimax tree over that
mixture. It is therefore both parameter-free and assumption-free: no domain-specific
tuning is required.

The corresponding drawback is that AIXI is uncomputable. Evaluating the universal mixture
requires simulating infinitely many programs, including non-halting ones, which is
equivalent to solving the halting problem. To circumvent this, I implement \textbf{AIXItl},
a tractable approximation that restricts the hypothesis space to programs of length at
most $l$, each simulated for at most $t$ steps. This preserves the theoretical guarantees
of AIXI for any environment whose generating program fits within these bounds, while
remaining implementable on ordinary hardware. Alternative routes to tractability
exist -- most notably MC-AIXI, which employs Monte Carlo sampling within the Solomonoff
predictor -- but these are beyond the scope of this project.

\section{Turing Machines and the Universal Turing Machine}
\label{sec:turing}

\subsection{Turing Machines}
\label{sec:tm}

A Turing machine is a fundamental model of computation. It is Turing-complete --
capable of expressing any algorithm that can be expressed at all -- which makes it the
natural substrate for the induction process described in later sections.

Formally, a Turing machine is defined by a 7-tuple
\[
  (Q,\ \Sigma,\ \Gamma,\ \delta,\ q_{\text{start}},\ q_{\text{accept}},\ q_{\text{reject}}),
\]
where $Q$ is the finite set of states; $\Sigma$ is the input alphabet; $\Gamma$ is the
tape alphabet ($\Sigma \subseteq \Gamma$), which includes a designated blank symbol;
$\delta$ is the transition function
\[
  \delta : Q \times \Gamma \to Q \times \Gamma \times \{L, R\},
\]
mapping a (state, tape symbol) pair to a new state, a symbol to write, and a direction to
move the head; and $q_{\text{start}}, q_{\text{accept}}, q_{\text{reject}} \in Q$ are the
start, accept, and reject states respectively.

The machine reads one symbol at a time from an infinite tape, updates its state and the
current tape cell according to $\delta$, moves the head left or right, and halts when it
reaches $q_{\text{accept}}$ or $q_{\text{reject}}$. Computation is deterministic: each
(state, symbol) pair has at most one applicable rule.

\subsection{The Universal Turing Machine}
\label{sec:utm}

A Universal Turing Machine (UTM) is a Turing machine capable of simulating any other
Turing machine. It takes as input a binary encoding of some machine $M$ together with an
input string $w$, and produces the same output that $M$ would produce on $w$. Crucially,
$M$ does not run as a separate process -- it exists entirely as data on the UTM's tape.
The UTM reads $M$'s transition table one rule at a time, determines the applicable
transition for the current simulated state and symbol, and applies it, updating a region
of its own tape that represents $M$'s tape.

For a UTM to simulate an arbitrary machine $M$, it must be possible to express $M$ as a
finite binary string. The convention for doing so is called an \emph{encoding scheme}. The
choice of encoding scheme is a design decision: any scheme that is unambiguous and
complete is valid, since the Invariance Theorem\textsuperscript{*} guarantees that
Kolmogorov complexity changes by at most an additive constant between any two reasonable
schemes.

\par\noindent{\footnotesize\textsuperscript{*}The Invariance Theorem is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:kolmogorov} on Kolmogorov complexity.}

\subsection{Encoding Scheme}
\label{sec:encoding}

The encoding scheme used in this project represents a Turing machine as a self-delimiting
binary string structured as follows.

\paragraph{Header.} The string opens with two unary fields. The number of states $n$ is
encoded as $n$ ones followed by a zero; the size of the tape alphabet $k$ is encoded the
same way. For example, four states and three symbols give the prefix
\texttt{1111\,0\,111\,0}. These fields are self-delimiting -- a decoder reads ones until
it sees a zero, requiring no external length information.

\paragraph{Field widths.} Once $n$ and $k$ are known, all subsequent binary fields have
fixed, computable widths:
\begin{align*}
  \texttt{state\_bits}  &= \lceil \log_2 n \rceil && \text{(minimum bits to index any state)},\\
  \texttt{symbol\_bits} &= \lceil \log_2 k \rceil && \text{(minimum bits to index any tape symbol)}.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Special states.} The indices of $q_{\text{start}}$, $q_{\text{accept}}$, and
$q_{\text{reject}}$ follow immediately, each encoded in \texttt{state\_bits} bits. No
delimiters are needed because the width is known exactly.

\paragraph{Transition table.} The transition function $\delta$ is stored as a flat table
of $n \times k$ entries in lexicographic order of (current state, current symbol) pairs.
The input pair is implicit from the entry's position; only the output is stored. Each
entry consists of three fields:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Field & Width \\
  \midrule
  \texttt{new\_state}  & \texttt{state\_bits} bits \\
  \texttt{new\_symbol} & \texttt{symbol\_bits} bits \\
  \texttt{direction}   & 1 bit ($0 = $ Left, $1 = $ Right) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Again, no delimiters are required. A decoder reads exactly
$(\texttt{state\_bits} + \texttt{symbol\_bits} + 1)$ bits per entry and reconstructs each
transition rule from the entry's position in the table.

\paragraph{Blank symbol.} Finally, the index of the blank symbol is appended in
\texttt{symbol\_bits} bits.

The resulting string is fully self-delimiting: a decoder parses the unary header to
determine all field widths, then reads a fixed number of bits at a time for every
subsequent field. No delimiters appear anywhere in the binary portion of the encoding.
This scheme was chosen for its compactness relative to a full unary encoding while
remaining simple to implement and verify.

\subsection{Worked Example}
\label{sec:encoding-example}

Consider the following Turing machine, which accepts any binary string of even length and
rejects any string of odd length:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  States    & $Q = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ \\
  Alphabet  & $k = 3$ symbols (indices $0, 1, 2$ -- where $2$ is blank) \\
  Blank     & index $2$ \\
  Start     & state $1$ \\
  Accept    & state $2$ \\
  Reject    & state $3$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The machine works by alternating between two non-halting states ($0$ and $1$) on each
input symbol, effectively counting parity. On reading a blank (end of input), state $1$
accepts and state $0$ rejects.

\paragraph{Computing field widths.}
\begin{align*}
  \texttt{state\_bits}  &= \lceil \log_2 4 \rceil = 2,\\
  \texttt{symbol\_bits} &= \lceil \log_2 3 \rceil = 2.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Header.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  States (4)   & \texttt{1111 0} \\
  Symbols (3)  & \texttt{111 0} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Special states.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \texttt{start = 1}  & \texttt{01} \\
  \texttt{accept = 2} & \texttt{10} \\
  \texttt{reject = 3} & \texttt{11} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Transition table} (12 entries, one per (state, symbol) pair in order):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \toprule
  Input pair & Output $(s', \sigma', d)$ & Encoding \\
  \midrule
  (state 0, symbol 0) & $(1, 0, R)$ & \texttt{01 00 1} \\
  (state 0, symbol 1) & $(1, 1, R)$ & \texttt{01 01 1} \\
  (state 0, symbol 2) & $(3, 2, R)$ & \texttt{11 10 1} \\
  (state 1, symbol 0) & $(0, 0, R)$ & \texttt{00 00 1} \\
  (state 1, symbol 1) & $(0, 1, R)$ & \texttt{00 01 1} \\
  (state 1, symbol 2) & $(2, 2, R)$ & \texttt{10 10 1} \\
  (states 2, 3)       & \multicolumn{2}{l}{unreachable from non-halting computation;} \\
                      & \multicolumn{2}{l}{filled with $(2, 2, R)$ as a safe default: \texttt{10 10 1}} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Blank index.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \texttt{blank = 2} & \texttt{10} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Concatenated encoding.}
\begin{verbatim}
11110 1110 01 10 11
01001 01011 11101 00001 00011 10101 10101 10101 10101 10101 10101 10101
10
\end{verbatim}

Removing spaces gives the final binary string:
\begin{verbatim}
11110111001101101001010111110100001000111010110101101011010110101101011010110
\end{verbatim}

This 77-bit string fully specifies the machine. A decoder reads the unary header to
recover $n = 4$ and $k = 3$, computes $\texttt{state\_bits} = 2$ and
$\texttt{symbol\_bits} = 2$, then reads 2 bits for each special state and 5 bits for each
of the 12 transition entries, reconstructing the original 7-tuple exactly.
